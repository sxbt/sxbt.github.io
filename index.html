McMaster Formula Electric

I joined McMaster's Formula Electric Racing team in my first month of university. I spent a year on the manufacturing subteam before being promoted to Drivetrain Lead, where I led the development of our custom planetary gearboxes.

Over my five years on the team, I've worked on a wide range of projects. I've helped manufacture four carbon fiber monocoques and one chassis mold. Each monocoque takes weeks of mold prep, carbon layup, resin infusion, and post-processing, and the mold meant machining foam positives and building carbon fiber mold halves around them.

As Drivetrain Lead, I led a subteam through the research and development of a custom planetary gearbox. I interviewed and selected new members, assigned responsibilities, and audited all deliverables, including calculations, CAD models, and engineering drawings.

Switched reluctance motors

I spent 12 months at Enedym Inc., a Canadian startup manufacturing switched reluctance motors: 4 months full-time, then 8 months part-time during my third year of university.

During my 4-month co-op term, my main task was redesigning motor and inverter components for Enedym's Actium product line as the company moved from early prototypes to mass production. I redesigned the motor housings to be extruded, significantly reducing cost and material waste compared to the previous CNC-machined parts. I then redesigned the motor end caps to fit the new housings, including rerouting the cooling channels and redesigning the seals.

Enedym kept me on part-time through my third year of university, when I worked on a new generation of inverter products, designing the housing, busbars, and other mechanical components. Across the 12 months, I also worked hands-on with three vehicles undergoing diesel-to-EV conversions, designing mounts and covers, installing Enedym products, and running test drives and troubleshooting.

Robot design competition

MECHENG 2C04 is a mandatory second-year design course where every mechanical engineering student at McMaster builds a robot for the same competition. Out of 37 teams, ours won: we scored the most goals (9, against a class average of 2.9) and tied for the most completed runs (10 in one minute, against an average of 4.2).

The robot had to drive between two lines and score a ping pong ball in a goal, completing as many cycles as possible in a fixed time. Teams could step in at the starting line between cycles to load a new ball or straighten the robot. Everyone had to use the same course-supplied servo motors, extra points went to low weight, overall design, and using as few motors as possible, and powered wheels were banned, with a wheel defined as anything whose arc covers more than 90 degrees.

We used free-spinning wheels to keep the robot's momentum, and drove it with four anchor-shaped legs designed to just barely touch the ground, pushing the robot forward and back. The launcher used a spring-loaded rack and pinion, with half of the pinion's teeth removed: as the servo turns, the teeth pull the rack back against the spring, then the toothless half lets it go. That turned a motor spinning continuously into a repeating firing motion.
