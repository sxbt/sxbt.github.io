McMaster Formula Electric

I joined McMaster's Formula Electric Racing team in my first month of university. I spent a year on the manufacturing subteam before being promoted to Drivetrain Lead, where I led the development of our custom planetary gearboxes.

I've been on the team for five years. In that time I've helped build four carbon fiber monocoques and one chassis mold. Each monocoque is weeks of mold prep, carbon layup, resin infusion, and post-processing, and the mold meant machining foam positives and laying up carbon fiber mold halves around them.

As Drivetrain Lead I also ran the subteam. I interviewed and picked new members, assigned the work, and checked every calculation, CAD model, and drawing before it went out.

The gearbox, year by year

2023Inherited design

I inherited this gearbox from the previous lead. It was the first one the team built after the pandemic, when a lot of earlier knowledge had been lost. Given that, it was an impressive assembly, but it had three main problems:

  • High rotational friction
  • Too long
  • Leaky gaskets
2024My first year as lead

My priority was fixing those three problems on a very tight budget.

  • On the inboard side, I replaced the gasket with an O-ring face seal. The O-ring sits in its own groove, so it's hard to seat wrong, and it's an off-the-shelf part rather than a gasket we had to make.
  • On the outboard side, the 2023 gearbox always leaked midway between the bolts, which meant the cap was flexing and the gasket wasn't being squeezed evenly. I switched from a very stiff aramid fiber gasket to softer Buna-N, doubled the bolt count, and checked the cap's stiffness with FEA. Buna-N is also more forgiving of rough sealing surfaces, which helps when a student team has to rebuild a gearbox somewhere far from a clean shop.
  • To cut length, we moved the brake caliper from in front of the gearbox to its side, so the brake rotor could sit right up against the gearbox face. That shortened the spindle, which cut the moment arm between the wheel and the bearings, and let the gearbox sit much deeper in the wheel, which gave us more freedom on track width. Overall it came out 29% shorter.
  • For friction, the 2023 design had plain lubricated washers at each end of the planet gears, and they tended to bind. The planets spin on needle bearings and float on their shafts, so in a corner their own momentum pushes them sideways into those washers. The bolts running through the gearbox also lightly clamp the gear stack, so the washers needed either a bit of play or near-perfect tolerances. I swapped them for thrust bearings, which take that sideways load and still spin freely.
2025Senior drivetrain member

I wasn't lead for the 2025 gearbox, but a few of its biggest changes came from me, based on what I'd learned the year before.

  • We switched the spindle from aluminum to steel. Aluminum has no true fatigue endurance limit, so an aluminum spindle has to be designed for a finite number of load cycles. That's fine for a car built for one season, but a steel spindle the same size has a much higher fatigue strength, so we get more margin for a bit more weight. Steel is also harder, so the rotary seal runs on a surface that doesn't scratch as easily. Scratches on the aluminum spindle were a leak risk.
  • We added hardened washers under the thrust bearings. In 2024 the bearings ran directly on the aluminum spindle, which was my mistake. Rollers need a hard surface to run on, and aluminum wears under them.
  • I also designed further weight reduction for the housing.
NextWhat we'd still change

This design isn't perfect. Some of its flaws are my own mistakes. I designed the 2024 gearbox at the start of second year, before I'd taken most of the core mechanical courses, so I taught myself a lot of it. Others come down to budget, where we know the better solution but can't afford it yet.

  • Replace the X-ring between the spindle and the housing with a proper rotary seal. The X-ring works, but it's where most of our friction comes from, and this is the biggest change planned for next year.
  • Use O-ring face seals on both sides. It'll take some redesign, but with low internal pressure, a face seal is cleaner than a gasket and leaves less room for mistakes during assembly.
  • Make new gears. Electrical problems outside our subteam have limited how much the last three cars have run, so instead of paying for new custom gears, we've pulled, inspected, and reused the originals every year. Their ratio isn't ideal for our current wheel size, weight, and motors, so we'd like a new set once the budget allows. We recheck the gears every year in Excel and KISSsoft, so every drivetrain member learns gear design properly.

Switched reluctance motors

I spent 12 months at Enedym Inc., a Canadian startup that makes switched reluctance motors. Four of those months were a full-time co-op, and the other eight were part-time during my third year of university.

On my co-op, my main job was redesigning motor and inverter parts for Enedym's Actium product line as the company moved from early prototypes to mass production. I redesigned the motor housings to be extruded instead of CNC machined, which cut both cost and wasted material. Then I redesigned the end caps to fit the new housings, which meant rerouting the cooling channels and redesigning the seals.

Enedym kept me on part-time through third year, and I spent most of that time on a new generation of inverters, designing the housing, busbars, and other mechanical parts. Over the whole 12 months I also worked on three diesel-to-EV vehicle conversions, designing mounts and covers, installing Enedym's products, and doing test drives and troubleshooting.

Mining

I spent 16 months full-time in mining. The first 8 were on site at Teck's Highland Valley Copper mine, and the next 8 were in consulting at BBA.

At Highland Valley Copper I was on the site services engineering team, designing chutes, liner packages, custom tooling, work platforms, and lifting devices. Mines move slowly, so none of my own designs made it all the way from concept to installation while I was there. Across different projects, though, I was involved in every stage, from design through fabrication to installation, and worked directly with fabricators and maintenance crews to sort out problems along the way.

At BBA I worked for mining clients on several continents, across different commodities and mining methods. I ran FEA in Ansys Mechanical and SolidWorks Simulation, went on site visits, and worked closely with electrical and mining engineers. Projects ranged from brand-new greenfield mines to existing sites going through major upgrades and retrofits.

Robot design competition

MECHENG 2C04 is McMaster's mandatory second-year mechanical design course. Teams of three each build a robot for a timed competition, and out of the whole class of 163 students, our team won. We scored 9 goals, against a class average of 2.9, and tied for the most completed runs, with 10 in one minute against an average of 4.2.

The robot had to drive between two lines and score a ping pong ball in a goal, completing as many cycles as possible in a fixed time. Teams could step in at the starting line between cycles to load a new ball or straighten the robot. Everyone had to use the same course-supplied servo motors, and extra points went to low weight, overall design, and using as few motors as possible. Powered wheels were banned, with a wheel defined as anything whose arc covers more than 90 degrees.

We used free-spinning wheels to keep the robot's momentum, and drove it with four anchor-shaped legs designed to just barely touch the ground, pushing the robot forward and back. The launcher was a spring-loaded rack and pinion with half of the pinion's teeth removed. As the servo turns, the teeth pull the rack back against the spring, then the toothless half lets it go, so one servo spinning in one direction fires the launcher over and over.
